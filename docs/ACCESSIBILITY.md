# Accessibility & Inclusive Design

## Overview

Echo is an assistive communication application developed during Capital Hacks at California State University, Sacramento. The project focuses on reducing communication barriers for individuals with dysarthria, including those with cerebral palsy.

Our approach prioritizes personalized speech recognition, accessible interaction, and user independence.

## Communication Challenges

Individuals with motor speech disorders may experience:

- Difficulty producing clear or intelligible speech
- Inaccurate transcription by conventional speech recognition systems
- Physical limitations when using touch-based communication devices
- Repeated misunderstandings in everyday conversations
- Reduced independence in social and professional environments

These challenges vary between individuals and do not necessarily reflect their language comprehension or cognitive abilities.

## Accessibility Design Principles

### 1. Personalized Communication
Adapt speech interpretation to individual pronunciation patterns, frequently used expressions, and communication preferences.

### 2. Minimal Physical Interaction
Prioritize large buttons, simple navigation, and reduced interaction steps for users with motor limitations.

### 3. Multimodal Feedback
Support both visual and auditory feedback, allowing users to interact through accessible methods.

### 4. User Autonomy
Preserve the individual's intended meaning and provide opportunities to confirm, reject, or correct speech interpretations.

### 5. Adaptive Learning
Use approved corrections and previous interactions to improve personalized communication assistance over time.

## Future Accessibility Improvements

- Hands-free and alternative input methods
- Customizable interface and text sizes
- Additional language support
- Improved speech recognition personalization
- Testing with individuals with speech disabilities and speech-language pathologists

## Our Commitment

Echo aims to make communication technology more inclusive by adapting to individual speech differences rather than expecting individuals to adapt to conventional speech recognition systems.

*Echo is an experimental prototype and has not been clinically validated as an assistive communication device.*
